# Why the experts disagree

> Ten real disagreements between the experts in this library — and the reason each one exists. The claim of this file: **almost nobody is wrong, and the disagreements aren't personality. They are projections of a small number of product variables.** Two experts standing at different coordinates of the same variables will give you opposite advice, and both will have the receipts.
>
> Use this file when two expert files give opposite instructions. First locate your product on the six variables below, then read the fight — the side you should take usually falls out of your coordinates.

---

## The six product variables that generate the fights

Hold this table in mind. Seven of the ten fights below are direct readings of it — and the file flags the ones that aren't (they're generated by stage or era instead; see the closing table).

## 1. When to start paid acquisition

**The four positions**: Matt Swulinski (Wispr Flow) — immediately, once tracking works and 50 conversions are banked. Rex Gelb (Cursor) — not before PMF. Elena Verna (Lovable) — year one is a death trap. Meltem Berkowitz (Deel) — only after the site is fast, findable, and has content to catch demand.

**The product anatomy underneath:**

Paid advertising has one structural requirement people forget: **the product's value must be demonstrable inside an ad impression, and collectible inside one short session.** That single requirement splits the four companies:

- **Wispr Flow passes the test perfectly.** Universal need (everyone types), value that shows in seconds (watch text appear as someone talks), simple tool, self-serve checkout. A 15-second ad *is* a full product demo, and the conversion loop closes the same day — which also means ad platforms get fast, clean purchase signals their algorithms can actually learn from. For this anatomy, paid is almost unreasonably effective, and Matt's "start now" is just reading his own product correctly.
- **Cursor fails the demo half — and its buyer compounds the problem.** Value shows only through *use depth*: you can't feel Cursor in an impression, you have to code with it. On top of that the vertical audience (engineers) converts from peers, not ads. Paid before PMF doesn't just underperform; it feeds churning users to the algorithm, which then finds more people like them — Rex's negative feedback loop is what happens when you force an ads motion onto a use-depth product.
- **Lovable passes the test but shouldn't take it.** Its value does demo in an impression — but every app a user publishes is already a branded, working advertisement — the product output is *visible* (table, column 6). When your product ships its own distribution, year-one paid spend doesn't just waste money, it **masks the signal** about whether the native loop works. Elena's "death trap" is specifically about products with loops: paid growth and loop growth look identical on a signup chart until the budget stops.
- **Deel fails it on session length.** The need is real but the purchase is compliance-shaped and sales-assisted: buyers research for weeks, compare, ask legal. An ad click lands on a website that has to carry a multi-touch journey — so the site, the content, the speed *are* the paid infrastructure. Meltem's "foundations first" is what "tracking first" means when your conversion happens over weeks instead of minutes.

**Locate yourself**: value demonstrable in an impression + collectible in one session + ad-receptive buyer + no native loop → Matt. Use-depth product or ad-averse vertical → Rex. Visible-output product with a loop → Elena. Sales-assisted, research-heavy purchase → Meltem.

## 2. How generous the free tier should be

**The sides**: Linear and Vercel run generous free tiers with no feature walls. Frank (5M-user AI app) and Phil Carter say cap it.

**The product anatomy underneath**: this fight is decided by *marginal cost* (column 5) crossed with whether a free user pulls other users in — either through visible output (column 6) or through a team-based buying motion (column 4).

- Linear's free user costs ~nothing to serve (column 5) and works inside a team (column 4: PLG → team), so every free user is a distribution vector toward a paid team plan. Vercel's free user costs real infra money but bounded — and pays it back by putting deploy buttons in public READMEs (column 6: visible output). In both cases the free tier is marketing spend with a built-in path to revenue.
- Frank's AI utility inverts all three columns: real inference cost per use, single-player usage (no team to pull, no visible output), and a universal-need audience that includes millions of people who will never pay. His free user is pure COGS with no distribution value. Phil adds the selection effect: generous AI trials specifically attract the heaviest consumers — the people whose inference bill is largest and whose intent to pay is lowest.

**Locate yourself**: one ratio — *distribution value per free user ÷ cost to serve them*. Collaborative or visible-output products with near-zero COGS: be generous. Single-player inference-burning tools: cap it. There is no philosophy here, only the ratio.

## 3. Cheapest good-enough model vs. frontier model at a loss

**The sides**: Gamma tested 20+ models in production and ships the cheapest that clears the bar. Granola burns frontier margin on purpose — "your marginal cost is my opportunity."

**The product anatomy underneath**: this fight is generated by **where the product's trust lives** and **how usage volume scales per user**.

- Gamma's output is a *draft the user finishes*. A deck that's 90% right is a usable deck — the human presenting it is the final quality layer, so model quality above "good enough" is invisible to the buyer. Meanwhile output volume is enormous (a universal-need product at 20K signups/day, each generating multi-slide artifacts), so per-generation cost compounds straight into the P&L. Anatomy says: margin play.
- Granola's output is a *record the user must be able to trust without checking*. One hallucinated action item and the product is dead to that user — quality isn't a feature dimension, it's the entire contract, and it's fully perceptible (you were in the meeting; you know what was said). Usage per user is bounded (there are only so many meetings in a day), so frontier cost has a ceiling. And the asymmetry only exists *because* Granola is small: Zoom and Google cannot run frontier models across billions of meetings. Anatomy says: quality play, subsidized until costs collapse.
- Phil Carter states the general rule from the advisor seat: **model choice is a growth decision, not just an engineering decision** — and note the fight only has stakes when inference is a real line item; a ~zero-marginal-cost product like Notion or Linear never has to pick a side.

**Locate yourself**: can your user *perceive* the quality difference, and does trust in the raw output constitute the product? Yes + bounded usage per user + incumbents structurally unable to match → Granola. No (human finishes the output) + high volume → Gamma.

## 4. A/B testing: discipline or evasion

**The sides**: Meltem rebuilt Deel's site specifically to A/B test value propositions fast. Karri Saarinen: "We don't really A/B test. I think A/B testing is often a way to avoid making real decisions."

**The product anatomy underneath**: the generating variable is **complexity + opinionatedness** (column 7), with traffic volume as the enabling condition.

- Deel is a complex, regulation-shaped product serving a *heterogeneous* horizontal audience — HR, finance, legal, across dozens of countries, each segment with different anxieties (compliance risk? cost? speed?). For a product like this there is no single correct message; the message space is genuinely unknown, and no founder's intuition covers it. Testing isn't indecision — it's the only honest way to learn a heterogeneous market. And Deel's traffic gave tests statistical power within days.
- Linear is the opposite object: a maximally opinionated product whose differentiation *is* its point of view. The Linear Method isn't marketing for the product; it's the product's reason to exist. A/B testing optimizes toward the average preference of your current audience — which is precisely the mechanism by which an opinionated product converges to blandness. Add that Linear's vertical audience (engineers) rewards conviction and smells focus-grouping instantly, and that its early traffic couldn't power a test anyway.

**Locate yourself**: heterogeneous audience + message uncertainty + traffic → test like Deel. Conviction-differentiated product + homogeneous taste-driven audience → decide like Linear. The expensive failure mode is running Linear's playbook without Linear's taste.

## 5. The follower floor for creator marketing

**The sides**: Ben Lang set a 50K-follower minimum at early Notion. Grant Lee runs 40+ micro-influencers and says big names are the wrong approach. Holly Chen tells B2B companies to use 1K–50K practitioners.

**The product anatomy underneath**: the usual explanation is platform era (YouTube then, TikTok now), and that's real — but two table columns generate the actual split: **time-to-value depth** (column 2) decides the content format and therefore the creator size, and **buying motion** (column 4) decides whether you need reach or precision.

- **Notion is a deep product with a blank-canvas problem.** Its value cannot be shown in fifteen seconds; it has to be *demonstrated* — a workflow, a setup, a life reorganized. That means long-form video, which means YouTube, which means creators whose economics support high-production full videos: the 50K floor wasn't a taste for big names, it was the minimum channel size where a full-length "how I use Notion" video gets made and found. The creator's demonstration substitutes for the onboarding the product didn't have.
- **Gamma is a 30-second product.** Its value shows in one screen capture: prompt in, deck out. That fits short-form perfectly, where feed algorithms distribute on content merit regardless of follower count — so a 3K-follower creator can land a million views, and what Gamma needs is *many authentic voices* rather than three expensive endorsements. Micro-fleet economics only work for products whose magic survives a 15-second clip.
- **Holly's B2B rule** ignores reach entirely because in a narrow-committee purchase, audience precision is everything: a 5K-follower newsletter written by an actual CFO out-converts a 500K generalist, since all 5K are buyers.

**Locate yourself**: deep product → long-form demonstration → fewer, larger creators (and expect to pay for production). Instant product → short-form → micro-fleet volume. High-ACV vertical → precision over reach, size irrelevant.

## 6. A year in stealth vs. launch on day one

**The sides**: Granola spent a year in closed beta with ~150 hand-onboarded users and killed its best demo before launch. openSEO posted in r/TechSEO with a working product on day one.

**The product anatomy underneath**: the generator is **interaction novelty** — a specific kind of product complexity.

- Granola was teaching a *new behavior*: a notepad that co-writes during meetings. Chris Pedregal's exact reason for stealth: "If we had launched that publicly, we never would have been able to switch it. Users would have learned a new behavior." For novel-interaction products, a public launch **freezes the interaction** — and if the interaction is the moat and it isn't settled, publicity is a liability, not an asset. Note the enabling condition from the table: Granola's wedge audience (VCs) was privately reachable at high density, so stealth cost nothing in distribution.
- openSEO was a *commodity replacement* — "open-source Ahrefs." The interaction is already universal knowledge; there is nothing to freeze. Its moat was community momentum, and open-source momentum only exists in public: every week of stealth is a week of not accumulating stars, contributors, and goodwill.

**Locate yourself**: is the core interaction novel and unsettled? Stay private until it's right — provided your seed users are reachable privately. Known category, known interaction, community moat → launch loud on day one.

## 7. Focus on 2–3 channels vs. always drill new wells

**The sides**: Matt — Meta + Google + lifecycle, nothing else. Yuriy Timen — you never know how deep your current wells are; always be drilling.

**Why they disagree**: this one is **not product-generated — it's stage-generated**, and it's worth being explicit about the difference. Matt is a solo operator in year one, where attention dilution is fatal and consumer-AI intent genuinely concentrates in two platforms. Yuriy ran Grammarly for nine years and watched channels he depended on saturate and reprice under him; at his tenure, channel decay is the fatal risk. Same law, different clocks.

**Locate yourself**: by stage, not by product. Focus until ~$10M ARR; explore in parallel after — with Yuriy's operationalization (a fixed slice of every quarter reserved for testing new wells) scaling up as you grow.

## 8. Channels you can't measure: invest or refuse

**The sides**: Rippling's Brandon Camhi on OOH — "make the investment because the logic makes sense... Intuition can carry you far." Matt and Rex — if tracking isn't clean, don't spend a dollar.

**Why they disagree**: partly stage (what share of budget is experimental), but there's a product variable hiding here too: **buying-motion length**. Rippling sells a multi-product B2B suite bought by committees over quarters — exactly the motion where mental availability among the 95% not-in-market matters and where attribution windows structurally can't see the mechanism. Matt and Rex run products bought by individuals in sessions — where measurement is possible, and skipping it is negligence rather than wisdom.

**Locate yourself**: if your buyer decides in one session, everything is measurable — behave like Matt. If your buyer decides over quarters in a committee, some of your best-performing spend will never attribute — behave like Rippling *at the margin*, after the measurable channels are funded, and use directional reads (brand-search lift, call-recording scans à la Clay) instead of faith.

## 9. Whether SEO can still win

**The sides**: Meltem (2023) — SEO plus cheap channels drove ~50% of Deel's growth to $300M. Elena (2026) — SEO is a baseline you must fund, "but it will absolutely not be the reason your business wins."

**Why they disagree**: half era, half product — and the product half is the interesting one. Deel's buyers have **obligatory queries**: "employer of record Germany," "contractor taxes Brazil" — questions that *must* be searched, that no social feed answers, and that regulation regenerates every year. Products whose demand expresses itself as obligatory search queries are search-native regardless of era — and notice this traces back to column 7: it's precisely Deel's *regulation-shaped complexity* that generates queries people are forced to type. Elena's prosumer AI categories are **discretionary discovery** — found through word of mouth, feeds, and AI assistants, not through queries anyone is forced to type. Then the era layer: by 2026, AI content flooded informational queries and AI Overviews cut clicks — which killed *undifferentiated content SEO* specifically, while data-backed PSEO (OpenRouter) got stronger because AI can't generate what it can't access.

**Locate yourself**: does your buyer's demand express itself as queries they are *forced* to type (regulatory, comparison, integration, price)? Then SEO can still be a primary engine — build it on data you own. Discretionary-discovery product → SEO is maintenance; put the offense where your buyers actually discover.

## 10. Attribution rigor vs. storytelling

**The sides**: Frank — closed data loop, paid conversion is the only north star. Lena Waters — "storytelling matters as much as attribution."

**The product anatomy underneath**: the same generator as fight 8 — **buying-motion length** (column 4) — crossed with **margin structure** (column 5), surfacing in a different decision.

- Frank's product: single-player, thin-margin, inference-burning, bought in a session by one person. Every misattributed dollar becomes compute cost with no revenue behind it, and the funnel is short enough that attribution is *possible*. Demanding it is competence, not culture.
- Lena's product: a complex PLG-to-enterprise system where a contract signed today traces back through years of community, templates, creators, and one colleague's workspace. No attribution window survives that chain, so a strictly-attributed budget systematically starves the compounding assets that actually built Notion. Yuriy's Grammarly data point is the bridge between the two worlds: "direct" traffic was really lagged paid and brand compounding — the measurement system itself miscategorizes long-cycle value.

**Locate yourself**: session-bought thin-margin product → Frank, without apology. Committee-bought, community-mediated product → Lena — while keeping Frank's discipline wherever attribution *is* possible, and grading the rest by credibility tier.

## Two fights that look real but aren't

**AI-generated creative: Matt's 5% cap vs. Austin Lau's 30-second ad factory.** Different layers, same rule. Austin's tooling generates *variations and copy* from human-authored foundations with human review; Matt's cap applies to *fully AI-generated video shipped as-is*. Both: AI multiplies human originals, it doesn't replace them.

**Brand advertising: Elena would buy OOH first with unlimited budget vs. Meltem calling early brand campaigns a waste.** Same law, two ends: Meltem's warning covers the first 6–8 months before bottom-of-funnel demand is captured; Elena's enthusiasm is explicitly a scale fantasy. Both treat brand spend as a maturity-stage amplifier.

## The meta-rule: three generators, six variables

Sort the ten fights by what actually generates them:

| Generator | Fights | What to check |
|---|---|---|
| **Product anatomy** (the six variables) | Paid timing (1), free tier (2), model cost (3), A/B (4), creator floor (5), stealth (6), SEO (9, partly — regulation-shaped products generate obligatory queries), attribution (10) | Locate your product in the table at the top |
| **Stage** | Focus vs. wells (7), unmeasurable channels (8, partly) | ARR and what share of budget is experimental |
| **Era / platform shift** | SEO's role (9, partly), creator floor (5, partly) | Date-stamp the advice; check if the platform economics still hold |

Every expert's advice compresses their coordinates into an imperative sentence, and the coordinates get lost in transmission. "Start paid immediately" carries an invisible suffix: *...for a universal-need product whose value shows in seconds, bought in one session, with no native loop.* "Don't A/B test" means *...when your differentiation is a point of view and your audience punishes blandness.* "Burn frontier-model margin" means *...when trust in the raw output is the product and your usage per user is bounded.*

The discipline, when importing any rule from this library: **reconstruct the suffix**. Find that expert's row in the table — who needed their product, how fast its value showed, who signed the check, what a user cost to serve, whether the output advertised itself, and how opinionated the product was. If your row looks like theirs, take the rule. If it doesn't, the rule isn't wrong. It's someone else's.
