## Core theses (5)

1. **⭐ The oil well theory**: treat your existing channels as oil wells you're currently drilling — you don't know how deep they go, so you must allocate time to drilling new wells in parallel
2. **⚠️ "The best content wins" doesn't hold** — yet he still chooses to make the best content (reasons below; the most counterintuitive passage in the whole piece)
3. **Evaluate channel expansion by "expected value"**: potential reach × potential impact ÷ startup cost × certainty of success
4. **Bring channels in-house one by one**, with a dedicated person driving each channel
5. **Building a relationship with the platform (e.g. Google) in exchange for beta features** is the most underrated asymmetric advantage in the early days

## ⭐ 1. The oil well theory (what he calls his single most important piece of advice)

Asked "what's your best marketing advice":

> "I would say don't sit or don't rest on your laurels. It's such a rapidly evolving space that, **you have to innovate or you're going to cease to be relevant**.
>
> I like to use the analogy... of **oil wells**. Think about your existing marketing channels as existing oil wells that you're drilling to sustain your community. But the problem is that **you don't know how deep those oil wells are**. It's very easy to just fall into complacency and keep drilling these oil wells, but before you know it, **you're going to hit rock bottom, quite literally**.
>
> That's why it's important to **split your time and diversify your time between drilling existing oil wells and looking for new oil wells**."

**Practical implication**: this isn't the old "diversify" chestnut — the point is that **you cannot know how deep your existing channels are**, so exploration isn't something to start once a channel dries up; it must be **normalized and run in parallel**.

**The institutionalized practice that goes with it**:
> "We **always carve out time in quarterly planning** to test new things… whatever we've figured out, we scale through automation as much as possible, so we can spend our mental and intellectual horsepower on **finding and testing new things**."

## ⭐ 2. The most counterintuitive passage on SEO: "the best content wins" is false

The host asked: many people say that to rank #1 you have to build the best page for that search term — what do you think?

**His answer (unusually blunt)**:
> "**I wish that were the case.** Unfortunately... **it is not the case today that best content wins, and I know this firsthand.**
>
> Why? Well, Google is incredibly sophisticated and continues to get more and more sophisticated with SEO, **but it still falls short** and there are a lot of bad actors out there where you'll see crappy content... sometimes that temporary means **like a year plus** where you'll see kind of suboptimal content being ranked pretty high up there."

**But his conclusion is not "then let's game the system too"**:
> "So, we pursue the strategy of **producing the best quality content that we can really stand behind that's representative of our brand — regardless of the outcome**...
>
> There are a lot of participants competing for the same keywords that **have less to lose**. They're okay risking being penalized or they have other intentions in mind. Unfortunately, many of them outsmart Google at least in the short term.
>
> **We don't get caught up in that. That's not the kind of a competition that we want to engage in. So, we're not really pursuing number one position at any cost.**
>
> ...because we understand that **every page on the grammarly.com domain, every ad creative that we run anywhere, represents our brand in a very integral and intimate way**."

**⭐ Why this passage matters**:
- He admits what most SEO content dares not admit — **in the short term, low-quality content really can win**
- But he gives a judgment **grounded in the balance sheet**: those players "have less to lose," while a brand can't afford the loss
- **This is the most honest framing of the "should we do black-hat/gray-hat" question**: not "it doesn't work," but "our loss function is different from theirs"

**⚠️ Recency note**: this judgment is from 2018. After the flood of AI-generated content in 2026, the "low-quality content wins in the short term" problem has only gotten worse. But note: **the real cause of death for content sites is usually not algorithmic penalties, but category competitors zeroing out your first-mover time advantage** — see the [Jasper case](../cases/jasper-seo-decline.md) (commonly miscited as "AI content got penalized"; the evidence doesn't support that).

## 3. Channel expansion methodology

**First bring the channels in-house one by one** (his actual path):
1. As a consultant, first proved out **affiliate / performance marketing** (a 3–4 month engagement)
2. First thing after joining full-time: **hire one person dedicated to driving affiliate** (already validated as profitable, with strong ROI)
3. Then brought **search and display** — outsourced to agencies at the time — in-house, hiring someone to drive each
4. Then brought **email marketing** in-house
5. Only after everything was in-house did systematic testing of new channels begin

**⭐ The expected-value framework for channel expansion** (verbatim):
> "Trying to approach channel expansion on an **expected value basis**:
> - **what is the potential reach and potential impact** of a channel we're considering
> - **what is the level of effort** to get started on there
> - **what is the level of certainty** that we will be successful
>
> The level of certainty was **primarily informed by channels where we had been successful in the past** (i.e. judged by similarity to channels that had already succeeded)"

**Org shape**: each channel runs like a small startup, with one "entrepreneur" driving it.
> He confirmed this closely resembles what Andre at Canva described: first run a channel yourself as an IC and validate the hypothesis is big enough → then hire a dedicated person → eventually build a strong bench of performance marketers, **so that others can go validate new territory too, instead of you being the only one exploring**.

## ⭐ 4. Building relationships with platforms (the underrated asymmetric advantage)

> "**Cultivating a close [relationship] with our Google account team allowed us to get access to a lot of beta targeting features that weren't available to other advertisers**, and the Google team knew us to be **very experimental** in that regard. We would jump on any beta features available to try to get a leg up on the competition. That actually served us really well because **we got a lot of early access**."

**The mechanism**: known for being willing to test → the Google team offers betas → targeting capabilities others don't have → an edge built during a period of lighter competition.

**Transferability**: any platform's (Meta / TikTok / Google) beta programs favor advertisers with **high experimental appetite + meaningful volume**. Actively cultivating the account-team relationship is a low-cost, high-return move.

## 5. Channel contribution across Grammarly's stages

**The early product (2012–2013) looked very different**:
- Web app only, **no browser extension** — using Grammarly meant going to grammarly.com
- **No free product** — only a credit-card-gated free trial
- But the conversion chain was healthy: strong visit→trial→paid conversion, decent retention, high ticket size
> "**That price point plus our retention is why we could make so many acquisition channels work.** And acquisition channels back then were far less competitive than they are today."

**Which channels carried the load long-term**:
- **Search and display** were the two long-term workhorses
- **Video / YouTube / TV** were important later additions — more upper-funnel, brand-leaning advertising, **but still ROI-measurable**
- **Lifecycle marketing / email** isn't an acquisition channel but is extremely important: once users install the free extension they live in the browser, so triggered emails are the key surface for complementing the product experience

**⚠️ A caveat on traffic mix**: the host cited SimilarWeb saying 60% of traffic was direct. He confirmed direct matters, but pointed out why:
> "Direct has risen significantly over the past two and a half years… this is where our brand awareness and the quality of that awareness really took off. **And as you can imagine, the paid advertising you run over the long term compounds — it delivers quite a big brand lift.**"
> → **In other words: direct traffic doesn't "just come naturally" — it's the lagged result of long-running paid advertising.** When doing attribution, you can't simply treat direct as free traffic.

## 6. Content strategy: from "writing with correct grammar" to "communication as a competitive edge"

**Topic selection doesn't start from keywords; it starts from narrative**:
> "it all comes from strategy. **What is the message that we're looking to get out there and who are we looking to reach?**"

The target audience at the time was shifting to **knowledge workers and business professionals** (product, acquisition, and content all aligned at once).

**⭐ The narrative they set out to build** (this is content strategy, not SEO tactics):
> "it's **not about writing with perfect grammar**, and it's not about writing error free for its own sake, but it's about **the integral connection between being a better writer = being a more effective communicator = getting ahead, getting the results and the outcomes**...
>
> A lot of people think writing well is something you were supposed to master in school that stops mattering in the real world — **we completely disagree**. So our content exists to build this connection: **better communication means a better you**."

**The lesson**: elevate the product's functional value (fixing grammar) into identity value (becoming a more effective communicator → getting better outcomes), and content gains a life beyond a tool manual.

## Counterintuitive takes (most worth remembering)

- **⭐ "The best content wins" is false** — yet brands should still make the best content, because **your loss function differs from the system-gamers'**
- **Chasing the #1 position at any cost is wrong** — "that's not the kind of competition we want to engage in"
- **You don't know how deep your existing channels are**, so exploring new channels must run constantly in parallel — don't wait for depletion
- **Direct traffic is the compounding result of paid advertising**, not free traffic
- **A good relationship with the platform account team gets you beta targeting capabilities others don't have**
- **Bring channels back in-house from agencies one by one**, each with a dedicated owner

Misapplying either goes wrong — drilling everywhere early = Matt's "ten channels all done badly"; still clinging to two channels at maturity = Yuriy's "hitting rock bottom."
