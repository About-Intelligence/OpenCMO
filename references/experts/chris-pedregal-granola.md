## Core theses (7)

1. **Don't launch until the product is meaningfully better** — a year of closed beta with ~150 users taught more than a public launch would, and preserved the right to pivot the core interaction
2. **Zero built-in growth loops can still go viral** — refuse growth hacks (no bot, no auto-emailed notes); serve only the user and let love do distribution
3. **Pick a wedge by signal density, not TAM** — VCs first, then founders ("if we could build a great product for founders, by default it would be a decent product for everyone else")
4. **The 2x2 for surviving next to giants**: frequent use case × important-to-user; only the frequent+important quadrant lets a startup's "we care more" beat distribution
5. **Your marginal cost is my opportunity** — small startups can run frontier models per-user when incumbents financially can't; cost collapse converts that into a quality lead
6. **Don't solve problems the next model release will solve** — even when users are screaming for them
7. **Retention comes from anchoring to an existing habit** (the calendar) plus privacy defaults that make the tool safe in any meeting

## ⭐ The stealth year: 150 users, features cut in half

From the Silicon Valley Girl interview (first-hand):

> "For the first year, the way we learned the most was literally by sitting next to someone watching them try to install it and use it, figure out everything that was wrong, go home, try to fix that, do it again the next day with a new person, and do that over and over and over."

> "I would definitely build in private or closed beta until I felt really, really secure that the product was meaningfully better than the competition."

Scale of the beta: "we had about 150 active users" — friends and friends of friends, each onboarded via a watch-them-install screen-share call plus a follow-up call three days later ("that was the highest signal").

Why not launch early — from Invest Like the Best (quoted in Startup Riders): they spent ~6 months on a real-time note-autocomplete interaction, then killed it:

> "If we had launched that publicly, we never would have been able to switch it. There's no way. Users would have learned a new behavior. We would have not retained that many users, and that would have been it."

Before launch they also cut half the product (MAD Podcast, via Startup Riders): "We looked at it all and we cut out 50%."

## ⭐ Launch and growth with zero growth loops, zero marketing

Public launch: May 22, 2024, Product Hunt + a tweet from Chris's own low-follower account. A GIF of notes fleshing themselves in caught tech Twitter; Guillermo Rauch and Nat Friedman retweeted. First day: ~500 installs.

> "It just started growing like little by little by little because we weren't doing any marketing. The crazy thing about Granola is all the old school AI note takers, they're all super optimized for growth hacking. Like at the end of the meeting, they'll send notes to everybody who was in the meeting whether they wanted or not... The fact that Granola was entering a space that was super crowded and had zero growth loops built into it, and it still grew virally, organically... I think it's a really strong testament that people are hungry for better software and better software experiences."

His advice when asked "don't think about marketing yet?": "I think so... if you don't have a product that itself can pop out and get noticed and loved, it just feels like a losing proposition."

**The wedge**: VCs — many meetings, formulaic notes, reachable in London, loud on Twitter, and every VC exposes founders and co-investors. Then, per the MAD Podcast (via Startup Riders): "As soon as we launched, we said okay great, now we're done with VCs. We're going to focus on... founders, just because we thought they'd be the hardest." By Series A, "over half of Granola users are in leadership roles" (Chris's own Series A blog post), with partners at Benchmark, Sequoia, Accel, USV taking pitches on it.

**B2B motion**: "It all started either bottoms up where it spread through the company virally, or the founder or CEO was using it... and said actually everybody should be using it. I think it's a great B2B marketing plan when you start with a consumer."

## ⭐ The no-bot decision: sacrificing the category's whole growth loop

Every competitor's visible meeting bot WAS its distribution. Granola refused (system audio instead), and Chris insists the motive was product, not stealth (Platformer):

> "The reason we made that decision was not so that Granola was hidden... The original goal was that we thought it was really important for Granola to always work... Slack huddle or a Zoom meeting or an in-person meeting... We also didn't like bots because we thought they're a really inelegant, dehumanizing force in a meeting."

The payoff: bots are banned from the most valuable rooms (boards, M&A, exec 1:1s); Granola isn't. And privacy became distribution-compatible: never stores audio, notes private by default —

> "Companies hate us for this — they're like, hey, there's all this content; why am I paying you money? And we're like, no... CEOs will ask us all the time... I want access to all the transcripts and all the notes... And we say no... If you have a backdoor way for someone in power to use all this context to make judgment calls in that way, that doesn't feel right to me." (Platformer)

His norm prediction: "I think we'll probably end up in a world where in work meetings, the expectation is that you are being transcribed by default, and you ask *not* to be transcribed."

## Retention machinery: dot plot, calendar anchor, 70% week-1

- **Numbers (first-hand)**: "70% of people return a week after installation" (Peter Yang Q&A); "Half of the people who try Granola still use it 10 weeks later, doing an average of 6 meetings a week" (Series A blog).
- **Why sticky**: "There's no bot joining your meeting or special UI to open — just an app on your computer that looks like a notepad... It integrates seamlessly into people's lives." Meetings are pre-scheduled, so notifications arrive at exactly the right moment and are "welcomed instead of seen as spam."
- **The dot plot** (Granola blog, taught by advisor David Lieb): a users × days grid of meeting counts that "mostly replaces analytics" — "for the entire year before launch, the dot-plot was the only dashboard on our wall." Analytics give answers; the dot plot generates questions about individual users' behavior.
- Chris does a user call daily; office screens stream live feedback: "When you're constantly immersed in user feedback, you develop an emotional sense of what matters rather than just analyzing metrics." (Peter Yang)

## The 5 hidden rules for AI products (Peter Yang Q&A, first-hand)

1. **Don't solve problems that won't be problems soon** — they refused to build chunking for long meetings and multi-language support; models solved both. "As a product person, it goes against every instinct to deny users something they're actively requesting."
2. **Go narrow, go deep** — beat Claude/ChatGPT by 10x on one use case; the 10x is often non-AI work (they built their own echo cancellation).
3. **Context is king** — treat the LLM "as an intern on their first day"; feed it who's meeting, roles, what each side is optimizing for, instead of brittle if-then prompts.
4. **Your marginal cost is my opportunity** — "As a small startup with fewer users, we can use cutting-edge models that would be financially impossible for big companies to deploy at scale." (Transcription fell ~$0.25/min → ~$0.02/min per Sacra — the bet that costs collapse before incumbents catch up on quality.)
5. **Build products that have a soul** — cohesion from intuition; "we interact with products similarly to how we interact with people."

**The competition 2x2** (Silicon Valley Girl): frequency × importance. Infrequent use cases lose to ChatGPT/Google by habit; frequent+important is "that corner where... if the product experience is even just like 10% better, that's reason enough for people to switch," and there the startup's only structural edge is "you can just care more."

## From notepad to context layer

Positioning has climbed a ladder, in his own blog language: "a steering wheel for LLMs" (2024 launch) → "second brain for your team" (2.0, May 2025) → "put your company's context to work" (Series C, March 2026: Spaces, personal/enterprise APIs, MCP). "Conversation transcripts are the richest source of context for what's happening across your company." On Platformer: "Our MCP usage is the fastest-growing chart that we have... Transcripts are this goldmine of context... There's this context exhaust that you're naturally putting out." On the moat question: "I think we are like 5 percent into this new technical revolution... transcription and meeting notes feel like the first little thing that's useful... If all we were going to be is a transcription and meeting-notes company, then I'd be really worried."

## Counterintuitive takes (most worth remembering)

- **A year of closed beta in the fastest-moving market in tech** — because public launches lock in your core interaction before you know it's right
- **They deleted their best demo** (real-time autocomplete) because reading live AI notes destroyed presence — the product's whole point
- **Zero growth loops on purpose**: no auto-emailed notes, no bot billboard — and the competitor's bot became Granola's conversation-starter instead
- **Refusing CEO surveillance requests is a growth strategy** — private-by-default made Granola safe to adopt bottom-up in any company
- **Run models you can't afford** — frontier-model quality while unprofitable per-user, betting on cost collapse (the mirror image of Gamma's cost discipline; both are deliberate model-cost strategies)
- **Forgetting is a feature**: "I go back less than you'd expect I would... Just knowing that you have it is very valuable" — he rejects driving that engagement metric up
- **Stare at raw data, not analytics**: the dot plot replaced dashboards for a year
- **Smallest possible wedge (VCs), then abandon it on launch day**
- **AI is most useful for small tasks, not big ones** — the thank-you note you'd never send, not the decision you're already thinking about
