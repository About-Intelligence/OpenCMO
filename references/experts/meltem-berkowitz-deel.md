# Meltem Kuran Berkowitz — Deel, Cheap Channels + SEO Carrying Half of Growth

**Who**: Chief Growth Officer (CGO) at Deel; employee #2 on the marketing team and #19–20 company-wide; previously led marketing at Bench Accounting
**Source**:
- Lenny's Podcast, hosted by Lenny Rachitsky
- Chinese deep-read: [(Strongly recommended) Deel's Chief Growth Officer shares internal product growth lessons](https://mp.weixin.qq.com/s/9iAjq5SEfuPWben7bPyqrg) (2023-09-21)

**Track record**: Deel is a global payroll/HR/compliance platform. When she joined in July 2020, ARR was under $1M; January 2021, $4M; end of 2021, $57M; April 2022, $100M; early 2023, **$295M** — 0 → $300M ARR in three years, **while staying EBITDA-profitable**. For a time, the fastest-growing SaaS in history (taking the title over from Ramp).

> ⚠️ **Recency note**: this is a 2023 interview, predating AI search's impact on SEO. Its SEO sections should be read alongside [Elena Verna](elena-verna-lovable.md)'s 2026 judgment (SEO is still the baseline but no longer the reason you win).

---

## Core theses (5)

1. **There is a non-skippable order before paid acquisition**: website → fast enough → discoverable by search engines → content to land on → **only then paid ads**
2. **The essence of B2B is "an active problem exists"** — people don't want to be sold to; they want to solve their problem
3. **Cheap channels = go where people ask questions and genuinely answer them** (and **never automate it**)
4. **The only passing bar for SEO**: after reading your article, is this Google search over?
5. **Early brand-awareness advertising in B2B is a waste** — spend 6–8 months punching through the bottom of the funnel first

**Results mix**: today roughly **50% of growth still comes from non-paid channels** (partners, SEO, etc.); early on that share was **80%–90%**.

---

## ⭐ The order of operations: her most central framework

Asked "how did you decide where to allocate resources early on," she gave a chain of questions that must be answered in order:

> "So the first question I would ask is, **do you have a website? Is it fast? Do search engines know it exists?** Okay, great. The next step is, **can people find it?** If they can't find it, you need to write content to make sure people can find it.
>
> **Only after you've answered all of those questions** should you consider: do I have the money? Can I put it into paid ads to make sure people visit my website? So scale step by step. **But if your website takes more than four seconds to load, you cannot run a successful paid advertising program.**"

She added: "This is how I would advise everyone to start — if I could be hired all over again, this is what I would do."

**Practical implication of this framework**: paid acquisition is an amplifier, not an engine. Scaling up spend before the foundation is laid only burns money faster.

**Companion: website and positioning**
- Deel's website was originally hard-coded; the first thing she did was **migrate it to a platform she could edit directly**, to enable continuous A/B testing
- Heavy testing of value propositions: lead with the problem vs. lead with the solution vs. save time vs. save cost — rapid A/B

**⭐ The one-liner test**:
> "In B2B, it's very easy to come up with statements that could easily apply to another business. **But if your one-liner could also apply to another company, don't let it be your one-liner.**"
>
> Like "we handle the complex stuff so you can focus on what you do best" — "you could apply that statement to 90% of B2B businesses, **which means it isn't good enough**."

## Cheap channels: go where people ask questions

**The underlying logic**:
> "Especially in B2B, most businesses get started because **there is an active problem**. So when you go to market and try to answer people's questions — **people don't want to be sold to; they want their problems solved**. And these cheaper channels are usually where people go trying to get answers."

**In practice**: she set up Reddit keyword tracking **on her first day** (using a "fairly janky helper setup" someone else had built) — still in use today.

**Channel list**: Reddit (first choice), Quora, X/Twitter, closed communities (Slack / Discord / YC-type — high barrier to entry but worth it)

**⚠️ You must check the audience-size ceiling** (a step many people skip):
> "If you're looking at a subreddit of about a thousand people, recognize the ceiling of that audience — **you're not going to win 5,000 businesses through that Reddit community, but you might win 10% of the people there who have this problem**."

**⭐ Never automated**:
> "**Never automated. Still true today. We will never automate interactions with people.**"

Early on it was her personally, other team members, **and the co-founders — who still do this today**. Many people on the team have access to these channels; whoever sees a thread first flags it.

**What counts as adding value**:
> The least effective approach is "hey, we've already solved this — check out our website."
>
> Adding value means **explaining how to solve the problem**: "Please genuinely treat this person like your friend and answer their question, for example: **Yes, this is doable. No, this isn't doable. Yes, you can do it this way, but you need to consider X, Y, Z. If you'd like to learn more, you can talk to us.**"

"The whole point of someone asking a question isn't to be sold a solution — it's 'I just need an answer.'"

**Add value only, no pitching — otherwise**: "You'll get kicked out very quickly. You can try not providing value, but you won't last long."

**How the contribution share changed**: early on, roughly **15%** of the sales funnel; now it's down to **under 5%** — not because absolute volume dropped, but because the rest of the funnel grew so fast; the number of people finding them through these channels is still growing.

**One extreme example**: during Covid, her previous company wrote a PPP (Paycheck Protection Program) explainer article so good that **the IRS website linked to it directly**, recommending taxpayers read it.
> "When you do your best to answer the question and others don't have the time to, whoever they are, they will send people back to you."

## ⭐ SEO: the traffic-light framework

**Keyword selection process**: the team lays out as many as **700 keywords** at a time (business-related, from tightly to loosely relevant), sorts them by search volume from high to low, then **judges search intent one by one**:

| Light | Meaning | Action |
|---|------|------|
| 🟢 **Green** | Very high intent — this person needs our solution | Do first, starting from the highest volume |
| 🟡 **Yellow** | 50:50 — might buy, might not buy anytime soon | Work from high to low |
| 🔴 **Red** | This person isn't going to buy; they're searching for another reason (e.g. a university student writing a paper) | **Usually don't touch it at all** |

**⭐ Only one criterion for whether an article passes**:
> "The most important thing is to think about this question: **is the Google search over?** If someone reads the article you published, do they go back to Google to read something else, or is the Google search over? Because what the search engine cares about is: I want to make sure this person gets their answer as fast as possible."

"Coming from that angle, it's actually easier to write things people want to read, instead of writing haphazardly and stuffing in so many keywords that people think 'I'm bored, I'm going somewhere else.'"

**Don't stuff keywords**:
> "I often compare keywords to your address — **it's how you tell Google 'please direct people to my website.'**"

**First look at what Google is currently serving** (a very practical step):
> Search "EOR" and Google serves **enhanced oil recovery**, not employer of record, because most people searching that term want the former. "If my team blindly went 'we're going to rank number one for EOR,' we would never rank, because Google isn't serving that kind of content."

**Google's "People also ask" box** below the results is a great place to understand "once someone finishes this search, what do they ask next."

**Tools**: score with **Clearscope**; bring language complexity down to a **fourth-to-fifth-grade reading level**.

**Content team structure** (about 8 people):
- Led by a content director (one of Deel's earliest employees)
- One person handles all operations (managing freelancers, ensuring briefs, fact-checking, on-time publishing, tracking)
- **Different people own content for different product lines** — "you need to build up expertise over time to write it properly"
- Recently added a team for new media formats (video, etc.)

**Output**: it used to be **10 brand-new pieces per week**; now it's **5 new + 5 updated** — because regulations change constantly, content published two years ago must also stay current, with a team continuously fact-checking. And it has to span multiple languages (originally English only).

**About half of the company's growth comes from this 8-person team.**

**⚠️ SEO is not for every company**:
> "If you're in a space where **people are searching for solutions**, then you should do SEO. But if you're a direct-to-consumer company selling lipstick or the like… people don't go on Google for that; they go to Instagram, they go find influencers."

**⭐ The biggest mistake is trying to take shortcuts**:
> "The biggest mistake people make is treating SEO as something you can try to automate. There's a lot you can do to save time, **but it never stops being time-consuming. To do it well, it will be time-consuming.** So people often give up, or they decide that doing the keyword research and executing all of these things one by one isn't worth it to them. That's usually where people lose the opportunity, **because they tried to take a shortcut. When you take shortcuts, you only create low-quality resources.**"

## Paid acquisition: concrete lessons

**Long-tail platforms** (the focus once the four or five big platforms are covered):
> "Individually, these platforms will never contribute a big enough share for you… but if you add them together, they diversify your lead flow, **up to about 30% of total traffic**."

Including: review sites, much smaller media outlets, newsletter ads, podcast ads.
> "When you run one standalone podcast ad, yes, you may not get 2,000 customers from a single podcast. But if you run 10 ads, it gradually adds up."

She points out these are often overlooked because **the setup is a hassle** — "it takes the same effort, but you need diversified lead sources." Deel would run very specific placements on sites with only 50K or 100K monthly visits.

**Creative fatigue is real**:
> "When you launch an ad that works for three weeks, people get tired of seeing it, and you need to keep refreshing it."

Deel's paid team **refreshes ads monthly** (Google Ads is the exception — those need to stay direct and to the point). Also make sure the messaging keeps up as the product evolves.

**⭐ The review lens: don't just count leads**
> "Look not only at how many leads your campaigns generated, but at **how many leads actually became customers, and how much money you actually made from those customers**, to understand where you stand on payback. Because usually marketers only tend to think, 'oh, this is a great lead channel, I got tons of leads from it.' But if you take that question to the sales team, they'll say: 'Yeah, I'm busy, but none of these leads convert.'"
>
> "It's very important to look not only at the volume you brought in, but at **that volume's journey through your business a year later**."

## ⚠️ Against early brand-awareness advertising (B2B)

> "I'm not a big fan of doing these awareness campaigns early, especially for B2B businesses. If you're a consumer founder, you can skip this part."

**Reasoning**: awareness campaigns take time, require a team for strategy and creative, **and they don't always work**. "You look back, you've spent an entire month, it hasn't resonated, and you've wasted a whole month."

**Her order**:
> "Because B2B businesses usually exist because there's a very real need, and there are plenty of people ready to convert, **you have to break into the bottom of the funnel first, and only then go to the masses**. But I promise you: before you start doing awareness advertising, **you'll probably need at least 6 to 8 months breaking into the bottom-of-funnel demand that's ready to convert today**."

"Early on it's usually a shiny, cool-looking thing that can end up being a waste of time. Because people don't even really understand what you do, and you haven't even figured out which message resonates."

**A positive example: Notion's out-of-home ads**
- The key is **timing**: they weren't done early, but at a **later stage** of the company's growth, when everyone in tech already knew what it was
- The ads showed **the product interface directly** — the goal was **reinforcing the message, not introducing itself to the world**
- "If they had done that from the very beginning, people would have said: 'What am I looking at?'"

## Partnerships: audience overlap ≠ effective

> "People often partner with any company that shares their audience. It's a decent starting point. **But just because you share an audience doesn't mean your audience turns to your partner for help when facing this problem.**"

**Deel's example**: VCs are important partners, because portfolio companies, once funded, ask their VC "what are your other portfolio companies using?" — **a scenario in which people genuinely go and ask**. Other tech companies might reach exactly the same audience, but **people never go ask them that question**, so the partnership won't work.

**The test**: whether **these people are seen as a credible resource for the solution you provide**, not just audience overlap.

## Product is the precondition for everything

> "While we're discussing all these ways to grow a product, **at the core there has to be a genuinely valuable product that people truly want to keep using**… **If the product doesn't meet expectations, no acquisition channel will work at all.**"

"Even if you're the best marketer in the world, even if your sales team does a great job convincing them to become customers, **if the product doesn't meet expectations — especially in the B2B world — people won't tolerate a bad product. They'll leave. And it will spread.**"

**⭐ An actionable signal — look at early team composition**:
> "When I joined Deel, most of the team was product and engineering. So that told me the core of this business was going to be solid. Then we built the supporting pieces — marketing, sales, and data — to support the product.
>
> **But if you're talking to an early-stage B2B product company and they have six salespeople and two engineers, their product is not going to be good for long.**"

An interesting flip side: in Deel's early sales conversations, people would say "I don't think you can do all of that; I don't believe you" — **they practically had to tone down their own messaging to be believed**.

## Hiring bar

**⭐ First question: which KPIs are you willing to commit to?**
> "If someone is only willing to commit to lead volume, **that's not enough**. They need to be able to commit to closed-won revenue KPIs, to truly show they care about the company's bottom line. So the people willing to commit to the full funnel are the best fit."

**⚠️ Don't worship big-company résumés**:
> "If your team is only 35 people and you're trying to hire a department head from a big company, you need to ask this question: **when did this person join that big company?** Did they join when the company already had 5,000 employees and had every resource from day one? Or were they actually one of the early employees who helped the company grow?
>
> Because the mistake people often make is hiring from an amazing company, **but the person isn't used to working with 10% of the resources they used to have. So they're unwilling to get down and do the dirty work.**"

**Deel's "little hand" concept**:
> "No matter who you are or where you sit in the organization, **you must be willing to do the small things, do the grunt work, and not shy away from it**. This matters when hiring at any level… If someone says 'I'll build a team for that' — sure, maybe someday you should, but that shouldn't be your first answer. **Your first answer should be 'yes, of course I'll do it.'**"

## Counterintuitive takes (most worth remembering)

- **If your website takes more than 4 seconds to load, forget about running successful paid ads** — a hard threshold concrete enough to self-check immediately
- **Never automate the cheap channels**; the co-founders still personally answer questions on Reddit today (against the "growth automation" tide)
- **Don't touch red-light keywords at all** — most people sort only by search volume, not by purchase intent
- **Early B2B brand advertising is a waste**; hit the bottom of the funnel for 6–8 months first
- **SEO is not for every company** (if you sell lipstick, don't)
- **Shortcut SEO equals no SEO** — "it never stops being time-consuming"
- **Audience overlap doesn't make a partner effective** — check whether they're "the one people actually go ask"
- **Judge whether the product will be good by early team composition**: 6 salespeople + 2 engineers = the product won't be good for long
- **Don't hire people who will only commit to lead volume**

## Why Meltem's views are worth referencing

1. **0 → $300M in three years while EBITDA-profitable** — most hypergrowth stories are bought with burned cash; hers wasn't
2. **Half of growth came from an 8-person content team** — a leverage ratio of enormous reference value for small teams
3. **She gives an order of operations, not slogans** — "fast website → discoverable → content to land on → then paid" can be self-checked item by item
4. **She's willing to say "don't"**: don't do early brand ads, don't assume every company should do SEO, don't automate community interactions
