# Matt Swulinski — Wispr Flow, One Person Carrying an Entire Paid Media Department

**Who**: Head of Growth at Viktor (an Accel-backed AI employee product); previously the first marketing/growth hire at Wispr Flow; before that, growth at Superhuman (later acquired by Grammarly)
**Source**:
- 20VC podcast "How to Build a $100M Growth Engine: Lessons from Wispr Flow & Superhuman", hosted by Harry Stebbings
- Chinese deep-read: [Shocking! Wispr Flow's growth lead drops bombshells](https://mp.weixin.qq.com/s/_Xin__GuiBlrh3HCRmDUdQ) (2026-08-15)

**Track record**: Wispr Flow's official hiring page states ARR went from $2M to $50M in 12 months. For the year and a half through December 2025, **all paid execution at the company was done by him alone** — a $1M monthly Google budget, hundreds of thousands of search terms, ads/experiments/landing pages — while simultaneously negotiating partnerships with 70–120 newsletters.

---

## Core theses (5)

1. **Start paid acquisition immediately** — "At the end of the day, paid is the fastest way to validate whether this product can scale"
2. **"The e-commerce playbook is the right playbook for SaaS"** — because in e-commerce every single cent has to equal a purchase or an add-to-cart
3. **"Distribution is the only moat"**
4. **"Creative is the targeting"** (after Meta's Andromeda update)
5. **90% of companies die before spending their first dollar** — launching without conversion tracking done right means letting the algorithm target people at random

---

## Timing: a head-on collision with mainstream advice

The host threw the mainstream position at him: paid acquisition is a dangerous drug, don't touch it early — do content, brand, and word of mouth first.

His answer was one word: **immediately**.

Not that you shouldn't do content — those things are just too slow. Write articles, go on podcasts, wait for reactions, do interviews — do all of it, but meanwhile on the paid side **you can test your messaging, creative, funnel, and positioning end-to-end in a single week**.

**But he set his own precondition** (often overlooked, and the reason his disagreement with Elena Verna is smaller than it looks):
> First get conversion tracking working and **accumulate 50 conversions** — the minimum threshold for the algorithm to say "I know what your target customer looks like." Then give it 2–3 weeks to read direction, 3 months to read results.

"Within two or three weeks you can tell: can I start printing money today, or do I have to go back and redo all of this."

## Channels and budget

**Do only three things**: Meta (video intent) + Google (search intent) + lifecycle ops (email/SMS/push)
> "With those three, you can go all the way to your first one million, ten million in annualized revenue."

On "we have to do TikTok, we have to do Reddit," his verdict is blunt: **ten channels each done badly won't help you**.

**Budget**: if you've raised $3–5M, $100K is a reasonable starting point, but what really determines the number is revenue per user.

**The most underrated channel: affiliate marketing**
- Note the difference from a referral program: referral lives in-product and targets your users; **affiliate lives outside the product and targets people who aren't your users yet**
- "You pay them a percentage of revenue, and they go proactively make content and reviews. At both Wispr Flow and Viktor, this has been the highest-ROI channel"
- At Viktor, **10%–15% of monthly acquisition comes from affiliates**, with payouts of 10%–15% of revenue
- "One affiliate brings in a company paying $10,000 a month, and that single deal earns them $1,500 a month; some of our affiliates make twenty or thirty thousand dollars a month"

## Metrics (two counterintuitive ones)

**Early on, look only at CAC**
> "Leave retention for the product team to worry about — as long as the users you bring in are high quality."

**An LTV:CAC of 1:1 is fine early on**
> "What you want is to get as many people as possible using and believing in your product as fast as possible. Don't go lower than that — lower than that and you're burning money."

The reason is density: open X on any given day and there are a hundred new products, five in your lane, and two that copied your entire website. **At that density, the only way to win is distribution.**

**Fully-loaded CAC** — especially important for AI products
> "Free-trial credits have to be added on top of your ad spend. If you're not counting it that way, what you're calculating isn't your true acquisition cost."

On the cost side, don't just count the model bill either: "What about your compute platform? Inference costs? All the things that support your service?"

**A trap specific to AI products**: Meta's and Google's algorithms **were never optimized for usage-based pricing**. The same new user might contribute $50,000 a year or $50, and the algorithm doesn't know what to do with that variance.

## Creative strategy (the piece he considers most critical today)

**Background: Meta's Andromeda update changed the targeting logic**
- Media buyers used to tune audience settings in the back end
- Now the platform's position is: **don't tell me who these people are — I'll analyze your creative and find them for you**
- → **"Creative is the targeting."** This wiped out the jobs of the media buyers who made a living tweaking ad sets in the back end; their work has been forced entirely into creative strategy

**Creative volume**
> "Say you have a $100K monthly Meta budget — you need **at least 400 to 500 new creatives a month**. Otherwise you'll hit a ceiling and get outcompeted."

**Three legs of supply**:
1. A creator program (rev-share on spend; each recruit produces 3–4 pieces a week; they have hundreds of creators)
2. Five external agencies
3. An in-house creative team

**80/20 holds "one hundred percent"** — asked whether 80% of results come from 20% of creators, he answered "one hundred percent": "that's how the algorithm works too — it finds the best ad and piles all the budget onto it"

**But winners don't come from copying templates**:
> "The best creative is often the one where **the camera shakes a little** — it looks like a mistake, but it grabs your attention."

**⭐ The most important point: it's never about a single creative — it's the entire creative package**
> "The point is never that one creative; it's **the package of all your creatives put together**. They have to be different. Different to the algorithm. Different ages, different genders, different scenes, different hooks. If you just hammer on the one thing that's already proven to work, performance will collapse. You have to keep making brand-new, thoroughly weird ideas, or your acquisition cost starts climbing."

**The limits of AI creative (a direct warning to anyone building creative-generation products)**
> "**AI's real superpower in creative is making variants fast.** But go look at the videos that are AI-made end to end — they're garbage. You can tell at a glance it's an AI video."

The ratio he gives: **at most 5%**, as a few odd, diversity-adding pieces in the account.
> "In every sense, creative is nowhere near the point where it can replace real humans."

## Conversion tracking: where he says 90% of companies die

**The e-commerce vs. SaaS gap**
- E-commerce has ready-made tools: install a pixel, plug in your ad data, and it tells you which conversions were double-counted between Meta and Google and who actually brought the new customers — **out of the box, installed in 15 minutes**
- SaaS is a completely different world: **nothing exists out of the box to fill this gap**, and every company has to build it from scratch

**The full chain of consequences** (he is very specific):
> "Say your conversion tracking is bad — users subscribe but don't get recorded, your match rate is only half. I go optimize for the subscribe action, but Meta has no idea who those people are. So it targets people at random, and your acquisition cost goes through the roof. Then you say: paid doesn't work for me."
>
> "**Most of the time it's not that paid doesn't work — it's that you never got the setup right before saying that sentence.**"

**Two technical metrics that must be maxed out**: Meta's match rate and Google's data enrichment score. Leave them unmaxed and "you might be losing half your people" — to the platforms, those people are ghosts.

## His AI growth engine (built with Claude Code)

**Stack**: Claude Code running locally + a folder system + a pile of skills and md files
> "I don't write code. I learned by doing. I wanted to automate this, so I asked Claude Code: how would you do this? **I asked it how to build an OS**, and that's how it got built."

**The fully automated newsletter partnership pipeline**:
1. Claude hooks into his inbox; scheduled jobs check at 9am, 12pm, and 6pm each day for new partnership requests
2. Once it identifies a partnership request, it **proactively asks for the other side's pricing, researches their audience, and handles the first round of negotiation**
3. **He does exactly one thing**: decides whether to work with them
4. Once the contract is signed, **his manual involvement ends**
5. The system automatically: ingests cost data → writes copy → sends emails → generates links → sets up conversion tracking → sends the full package to the partner → collects data and computes performance → decides against preset thresholds whether to continue

**Why this is more than an automation script**:
> "Because it knows how every single link performed, it can judge that this copy worked and that one didn't. So the next time it generates copy, it's based on the entire history. **That's self-improvement.**"

**Daily discipline**: 90–95% of each day's work passes through AI. The reason isn't speed —
> "Because that way I can give it feedback and close the feedback loop. So it gets a little better every day, a little better every day."

**⭐ The easiest trick to steal outright: the "session wrap-up"**
- Hook Claude Code up to an Obsidian vault and write a skill that triggers as a work session winds down
- The skill distills the session: what got done, how the problem was framed at the time, whether the goal was reached, what's left unfinished, what was learned — then writes it into Obsidian
- Why the extra step: "**Everything is stored in the model's memory, but you can't get at it**"
- The effect, which he calls "a massive cheat": "It'll tell you: a week ago you were working on this, you hit a wall here; today you did it again, and you solved it"
- Because the AI builds the note graph itself: if you've run the same kind of creative on Meta in two different places, **normally you'd forget and so would the system**; with this layer it remembers and connects them for you

**His verdict on all this (the most forward-looking line of the whole episode)**:
> "Whether you can write down every little thing you build, tune, and change may be a person's most valuable resource. Just as social capital was once the most important thing, in the coming years **the AI output stored on your computer is many people's treasure**."

**Another thing he uses himself**: a meeting recorder captures every meeting; at the end of the day it all gets ingested to answer one question — **what did I promise on calls today that made it into no to-do list?** The list becomes tomorrow's to-dos.

## Hiring bar: "systems thinkers"

**The bar a year ago**: ten years of Meta buying experience, hundreds of millions of dollars spent, one of the best media buyers on the planet.

**Now**:
> "I still want that. But he doesn't have to be the strongest in history. If he's AI-native, or a systems thinker who can decompose how his own job actually runs, then that person plus experience will run circles around someone who has only experience and isn't AI-native."

**The line he flags as a "hot take"**: "A lot of teams are trying to make themselves AI-native, and they are failing." The reason is the people: **the people in those seats aren't systems thinkers, so they don't actually know what they're doing**.

**What a systems thinker is** (his definition is concrete):
> "Someone who can step back from a specific task, keep one degree of distance from it, and ask: in my role, what are the things I do every day? How do they interlock? Which part is boring grunt work? Which part is reporting? Draw it out — understand how your own job runs as a system."

"A lot of people go ask ChatGPT a question, finish, and then go back to doing their job the old way."

**His test for people managers**: run this thought experiment on yourself — **what would it take to fully automate my job?** It's fine if you can't; the point is that "if you ask a lot of people this, you'll find they don't really understand their own jobs and can't say what the inputs and outputs of their role actually are."

**How he interviews for it** (a clever question):
> Not "how do you use AI at work," but "**in your personal life, is there any system running on AI that makes you a better person?**" — everyone can recite the work answer; the life answer can't be faked.

**Example of a bad answer**: "I have a project conversation with context in ChatGPT, and I chat with it."
> His verdict: factually not wrong — that's how the memory and context layers should be used. "**But chatting with the thing is not a workflow.**"

**The two layers he's actually testing**:
1. **The feedback loop**: AI spits a pile of garbage at you — what do you do? Most people say "this is garbage, fix it," get more and more annoyed with each revision, and end up pulling the doc over and rewriting it themselves. "If you won't go through the pain of giving it feedback, that's the first feedback loop I'm testing for — you don't have one"
2. Whether they can build the kind of self-improving loop described above

**How many candidates meet the bar**: **less than 1%**.

**The future of team headcount**: at a decent-sized SaaS company, the influencer marketing team might be three to five people; tomorrow it's **one strong person with a full fleet of agents**.
> "Imagine a hundred thousand influencers posting about you every month. If you have an agent system, and you know how to filter the data and where to step in, you can do it."

His own timeline: "Right now it's still 80% of the work by hand, 20% by agents. My bet is that in three years the company will look like a board of directors: 20% of the work is strategy and thinking, and the remaining 80% of execution goes to agents."

**The flip side, in his own words**: this isn't simply laying off a batch of people. The former A-players are becoming S-tier, and the former B-players are becoming D-tier — the crack in the middle is being torn open. His advice to founders isn't to fire from a list, but: "just step out for a while and come back — who has turned themselves into a 10x person will be obvious at a glance."

## ⚠️ Boundary: the known failure point of this method

This one matters especially to us, because the protagonist admits it himself — it isn't outside criticism.

His one-person system **broke when handed to a team**. In the conversation he mentions that after building the system, he had to figure out how to hand over control of that folder so others could use the workflow too. His new employer's announcement of his hire put it even more bluntly:

> He single-handedly ran a channel that used to require a department, **then he tried to turn it into a system a team could use, and the day he let the second person in, it broke**.

**This overturns nothing he said, but it draws the boundary clearly: a one-person system and a team system are two different things.**

He also disclosed something honest: he was an early Claude Code user, starting in September 2025, **back when it wasn't good yet** — "It made mistakes constantly. The copy was bad, things would break. Those were very early days. But I'm a tinkerer."

> This line may matter more than all the tactics: he didn't wait for the tool to mature before getting on board — he decomposed his workflow while the tool was still rough, so that the day the models got good, his system was already there.

## Counterintuitive takes (most worth remembering)

- **Start paid acquisition immediately** — in direct conflict with Rex Gelb's "don't spend before PMF" and Elena Verna's "the first year is a death trap" (see comparison below)
- **An LTV:CAC of 1:1 is fine early** — don't chase 3:1
- **Don't look at retention early** — "leave retention to the product team, as long as you bring in high-quality users"
- **AI creative capped at 5%** — a conservative judgment on AI-generated creative, coming from an AI-era growth lead
- **Ad performance doesn't hinge on your single best creative but on the diversity of the creative package**
- **"Chatting with ChatGPT is not a workflow"** — the line he uses to screen out most self-described AI-native candidates
- **Affiliate marketing is severely underrated**, with ROI above most paid channels

## Why Matt's views are worth referencing

1. **An extreme but real sample**: a year and a half, one person, a $1M monthly budget — the trade-offs made under those constraints expose what is truly necessary better than big-team experience does
2. **He disclosed his own failure boundary**: the one-person system broke when handed to a team — few practitioners are willing to talk about this
3. **He is a hands-on sample of AI-native growth**, not a concept evangelist: the Claude Code OS, session wrap-ups, and feedback loops are practices you can copy directly
4. **His judgment on AI creative is conservative** (at most 5%), while he himself is among the most aggressive AI users — that contrast makes the judgment more credible
