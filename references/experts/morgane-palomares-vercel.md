## Core thesis: replace SDRs with Product Advocates — a dual mandate drives PLG→Enterprise

The core tension in Vercel's growth model:
- The users are **developers** (deeply allergic to anything sales-y)
- The goal is **enterprise contracts** (which require high-touch sales)

Morgane's answer: **Product Advocates = growth roles with technical backgrounds** who do no cold outreach, and instead provide technical help and surface success stories.

---

## The Product Advocates model

### What is a Product Advocate

| Role | Traditional SDR | Product Advocate |
|------|---------|-----------------|
| Background | Sales | Technical (bootcamp, CS)|
| Day-to-day | Cold email, phone calls | Technical support, use-case expansion |
| Trigger | Entry into the funnel | Product-usage signals appearing |
| Goal | Book a demo | Help users succeed → natural upgrade |
| Developer reception | Seen as harassment | Seen as a valuable contact |

### The dual-mandate design

Product Advocates are measured on both:
1. **Self-serve revenue**: helping Pro-plan users expand usage
2. **Enterprise pipeline**: identifying accounts with enterprise-purchase potential

This solves the classic PLG org problem: Sales thinks PLG users are "low quality," while Growth thinks Sales is "poaching" its users. Product Advocates serve both goals at once, so the standoff dissolves.

## Key insights

### 1. The intent-signal toolchain

The signal-identification stack Morgane uses:

```
Product usage data (internal)
    +
Koala (behavior + intent-signal aggregation)
    +
Clay (automated outreach + data enrichment)
    +
Common Room (community signals: GitHub Stars, Twitter mentions)
    ↓
Product Advocates receive a priority-ranked action list
```

**Core principle**: don't rely on manual scanning — let the signal system push the most contact-worthy accounts to the front.

### 2. The minefield of "cringey" developer marketing

> "Developers will tell you exactly what they think of your marketing. And they will publicly roast you."

Developer communities are hypersensitive to anything that smells like marketing; the following instantly triggers a negative reaction:
- Pseudo-technical articles (SEO filler with no real technical depth)
- Inflated performance/reliability claims (engineers will directly verify and rebut them)
- "Enterprise-grade" language (developers see it as empty words with no substance)
- Having non-technical people write developer-facing content

**Developer marketing that works**:
- Technical people writing technical content, with sharp opinions allowed
- Open-source contributions → community credibility
- Engineering blogs (real war stories, not PR copy)
- GitHub project quality > Twitter follower count

### 3. Running freemium at 100K signups/month

At its peak Vercel added 100K+ new signups per month, mainly from:
- **The Vercel Deploy button** (one-click deploy from GitHub READMEs): the single highest-ROI acquisition source
- **Framework integration** (Next.js is made by Vercel; framework users are natural prospects)
- **The template ecosystem**: users search "Next.js starter template" → find Vercel templates → sign up

Lots of signups ≠ lots of revenue; Morgane's key task is finding the ~500 accounts with enterprise value among the 100K.

### 4. Signals for the PLG-to-Enterprise transition

The expansion signals Morgane uses (ranked by value):
1. Team members > 5, all actively using the product (org penetration)
2. Monthly traffic/build counts exceeding the Pro plan's ceiling (usage hitting the cap)
3. Job postings containing the keyword "Vercel" (hiring people to maintain Vercel deployments)
4. Funding/IPO news (purchasing power spikes)

### 5. Pricing design: let price discovery happen inside the product

> "The best time to have a pricing conversation is when the user is already getting value, not before."

Vercel's pricing path:
- Free: personal projects, no credit card required
- Pro: $20/month, accelerating the first payment
- Enterprise: fully custom, SAs involved

The key design: **the Pro plan is an "overage-driven natural upgrade,"** not the result of a sales push. When a user's builds/bandwidth naturally exceed the free limits, upgrading is easier than churning.
